Middlesex University: Menopause at work - raising awareness of symptoms and breaking the taboo around conversations

At a glance
Employer name Middlesex University
Employer sector Education
Number of employees 1,000 - 4,999
Action category Menopause
Summary of action and implementation Middlesex University implemented a series of targeted initiatives – including a campus-wide poster campaign, peer support sessions, and updated guidance for staff and line managers – to raise awareness of menopause symptoms and encourage workplace conversations. Prior to the project, 60% of surveyed staff reported they would never speak to their manager about menopause. Following the rollout, 67% felt their managers were open to discussing support options. The initiatives successfully helped normalise conversations around menopause and improved awareness of available internal resources across the organisation.

Background Information

Middlesex University understands that the menopause affects every employee in an organisation in some way, whether they are going through the menopause themselves or working with a colleague who is.

58% of our core workforce identify as female. 31% of female staff are between the ages of 45 and 55, which is the typical age of onset for the menopause. To ensure that staff going through the menopause receive appropriate support, and managers are informed about the menopause and how to discuss the condition with their team members, we created a series of initiatives aimed at raising awareness of the menopause and how to talk about it in the workplace.

The university currently holds a Bronze Award for the Athena Swan Charter for gender equality. Advancing menopause awareness has been a key achievement of our action plan and is a central component of our progression towards the Silver Award.

What they wanted to achieve

The goals of the project were to:

  • raise awareness of menopausal symptoms
  • highlight existing support available to staff
  • improve our internal support offer in response to the staff needs identified

Research by academic staff Dr Britta Stordal and Dr Bianca Stumbitz found that Middlesex University staff had a lack of awareness of some menopausal symptoms that have an impact at work, such as lack of sleep, brain fog as well as anxiety and depression. There were also gaps in knowledge around available internal and external support options.

If women experienced more well-known symptoms of menopause, such as hot flushes, they understood that the primary issue was menopause and some chose hormone replacement therapy to alleviate their symptoms. In contrast, if the primary symptoms were mental-health related, often these would not be associated with menopause, and they would be treated on the mental-health path. The following quote from one of the focus-group participants also illustrates the strong taboo and stigma of discussing menopause at work:

“My brain fog was so bad that I was actually afraid… that anxiety was so bad that I thought that I was going to be fired because I was incompetent, and I thought I was losing my mind… and I honestly thought that I had lost me. I felt like I was curling up into a little ball. […] I never spoke to my manager about it. […] I thought I would lose my job if I said anything about this. ” (FG1-3, female)

The support initiatives were aimed at all staff at the university, including men and pre-menopausal women. A poster campaign and a menopause support group were developed, as well as updates to our menopause guidance and training sessions. Success was measured by:

  • comparing results of staff surveys before and after the menopause campaign
  • interviews with staff
  • collating issues raised during risk assessments, particularly workstation assessments

How they did it

Alisha Gohil (Equality, Diversity and Inclusion Advisor) led the design of the menopause poster in collaboration with the academic staff. The poster was used in female toilets and kitchen spaces across campus. It was also shared during induction sessions and all-staff events, such as the healthy campus week fair.

The poster featured the slogan: ‘Could it be menopause?’, highlighting that symptoms of the menopause are not just hot flushes. It shared a more detailed list of symptoms with links to further information and support pathways. Placing the posters in areas with high staff traffic was a starting point for engaging staff in workplace conversations about the menopause and breaking down the taboo of discussing symptoms and support options.

Alisha also led staff ‘Pause for Menopause’ sessions, paired with a MS Teams channel for colleagues to join and stay connected between sessions. The focus of the ‘Pause for Menopause’ sessions was to give staff from across the organisation a confidential space to connect, ask questions and share their experiences about the menopause. Sessions have continued monthly through a mixture of online and in-person events to ensure accessibility and inclusivity.

The collaboration between HR and academic staff continued through updating the organisation’s menopause guidance. A broader list of menopause symptoms was included (for example, brain fog, fatigue, depression) and noted that symptoms can last for 4 to 8 years. There was a shift in tone of the document with “breaking down the taboo” and “embedding culture change initiatives” as the focus.

The guidance for managers and how to have conversations about the menopause was refined with more practical ways to talk about symptoms and support at work. The guidance document linked to the newly launched reasonable adjustments policy and passport, ensuring that staff had more detail on potential adjustments available to manage menopausal symptoms. These include:

  • lighting and temperature adjustments in offices
  • regular short rest breaks
  • flexible working options, where appropriate

The outcome

In March 2023, we asked staff about their willingness to have conversations about menopause in the workplace. We found that the majority of participants (60%) would never speak to their line manager about menopausal symptoms. In December 2025, after the introduction of the poster campaign, ‘Pause for Menopause’ sessions and updated menopause guidance, another survey was conducted. We found that the majority of staff (67%) felt that their line managers were now open to discussing workplace support for menopause.

Staff induction and training sessions were updated to include slides on:

  • what the menopause is
  • what support is available
  • how to access internal and external support

These updates have been received very positively, with managers and colleagues taking note of how to start discussions about the menopause in their offices.

Qualitative feedback was collected and included the following statement:

“This information is not directly relevant to me but I’ve taken notes because I can go and share this with colleagues so they know that support is available for the menopause and I can talk about it.” – Male staff member who participated in ‘Your Wellbeing at MDX’ training session

“It was a great session, just to know that there is an opportunity to share with others was really valuable.” – Female staff member who participated in Pause for Menopause

“A younger female colleague and I saw the poster in our office, and we had a conversation about what the menopause is and my experience of it.” – Female staff member undertaking Staff Risk Assessment (DSE Workstation assessment).

Advice for other organisations

Embedding equality, diversity, and inclusion is a core principle of Middlesex University’s Strategy 2031. The university will continue to build on the positive outcomes achieved through these menopause awareness initiatives.

By shaping initiatives based on direct qualitative and quantitative feedback from surveys, focus groups, and training evaluations, we have ensured that our offer is meaningful and generates sustained engagement. While some trial and error and fluctuation in engagement is to be expected, organisations should remain flexible to tailor menopause support initiatives for a diverse and evolving range of needs.
