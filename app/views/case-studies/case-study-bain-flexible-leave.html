Bain & Company UK: How Bain enhanced its family leave policies to support parents and carers

At a glance
Employer name Bain & Company UK
Employer sector Consultancy
Number of employees >20,000
Action category Gender Pay Gap
Summary of action and implementation Bain & Company UK reformed its parental leave and career support frameworks to create an equitable experience for all parents and carers. To address gender disparities and reduce parenthood-related attrition, Bain equalised leave entitlements, introduced pre-leave "ramp-down" measures, and provided ongoing HR and peer support, including virtual clinical assistance and emergency backup childcare. Post-leave measures feature phased re-entry and flexible options. These comprehensive steps normalise shared caregiving, support career retention, and help the firm advance its diversity and representation goals.

Background Information

Bain & Company is one of the world’s leading global management consulting firms. It partners with businesses and organisations across the private, public, and not-for-profit sectors. This case study focuses on Bain & Company's UK business, which operates from its London office and employs approximately 1,200 people.

52% of staff in the London office are women. But, as is common in professional services, the senior consulting team has a higher percentage of men. Bain has set a target of achieving 45% women in client-facing senior management roles and, as of September 2025, has reached 40% representation – up from 28% in 2020. Reducing the gender pay gap and improving female representation at senior levels are central to the firm's broader diversity, equity and inclusion (DE&I) strategy.

The professional services sector has long grappled with a pattern where unequal parental leave policies and inconsistent support reinforce career trajectories that are different by gender. When primary carer leave is predominantly taken by women and secondary carer leave is brief or underused by men, the career costs of parenthood fall disproportionately on women, with knock-on effects for gender representation at senior levels. In a firm where the 10-year journey to a partner role is the central career pathway, the transition to parenthood represents one of the most significant moments of attrition risk for women.

Before formalising its approach, Bain recognised that its support for employees around parental leave was uneven. It varied between individuals and was not clearly set out in a way that gave employees confidence about what to expect. The firm committed to developing a more structured, equitable, and consistently applied framework for family support.

What they wanted to do

Our goal was to ensure that all parents and carers – regardless of how they came to parenthood – received consistent, equitable support before, during, and after parental leave. A central ambition was to ensure taking parental leave does not adversely impact career progression, and to make the experience of returning to work as smooth as possible.

The firm wanted to target the full journey:

  • the run-up to leave
  • the period of leave itself
  • the return to work

It also sought to reduce the reliance on individual managers' discretion by putting clear entitlements and processes in place, ensuring a fair and consistent experience across the organisation.

How they did it

The foundation of our approach was an equalised parental leave policy, deliberately designed to support all types of parents and carers consistently and to reduce the disparity between primary and secondary carers. By making entitlements equal, we aimed to make it easier for partners and secondary carers to take meaningful leave, distributing the career cost of parenthood more evenly.

In the period leading up to leave, Bain introduced a ‘ramp-down’ process to ease the transition. This could include:

  • reducing or pausing business travel
  • offering hybrid working or temporary flexibility in working patterns
  • adjusting workload and hours where practical

This helped employees wind down responsibilities gradually rather than leaving abruptly, reducing stress and supporting a more positive experience of the pre-leave period.

A dedicated HR contact remained available throughout the leave period for any queries, providing continuity and reassurance. Bain also introduced a buddy system, pairing individuals with colleagues who had recent experience of parental leave, offering informal peer-to-peer support. The wider parents network provided another channel for shared experience and guidance. For clinical and practical support from early pregnancy through to toddler stages, employees had access to the Peppy app, offering one-to-one virtual support from practitioners such as midwives, health visitors, sleep consultants, and lactation consultants, alongside expert events and structured courses.

On return to work, Bain supported phased re-entry tailored to the individual's role and circumstances, with reintegration plans that prioritised training and refresher needs. Flexible working options remained available, and emergency back-up childcare was introduced to provide a safety net when primary care arrangements broke down – a practical but significant intervention in reducing the day-to-day pressure on working parents. The employee assistance programme also offered confidential support for the emotional transition to parenthood and back into the workplace.

One of the main challenges in implementing these changes was ensuring consistency. This involved making sure that the quality of support an individual received did not depend on which manager or HR partner they happened to be working with. This was addressed by:

  • formalising entitlements in policy
  • training managers on their responsibilities
  • establishing the dedicated HR contact as a consistent point of accountability throughout the leave journey

The outcome

Bain UK now offers one of the more comprehensive family support packages in the professional services sector. Our package covers the full parental leave experience rather than treating leave itself as the only moment of intervention. The equalised policy has helped to:

  • normalise parental leave as a shared responsibility
  • reduce the perception that it primarily affects women
  • reinforce the message that caregiving is a shared commitment
  • encourage more balanced uptake across all parents and carers

The combination of peer support through buddies and the parents network, clinical support through Peppy, and the practical safety net of emergency backup childcare has given employees greater confidence that parenthood does not have to mean a step back in their careers. Phased returns and reintegration plans have helped reduce the disruption of coming back to work, making it more likely that talented employees remain with the firm through this transition.

Advice for other organisations

Firms in the professional services sector looking to improve their family support offer should begin by reviewing whether their parental leave policies treat all types of parents fairly. Unequal policies embed gendered assumptions and make it harder for secondary carers to take meaningful leave. This in turn keeps the career cost of parenthood concentrated among women.

Look beyond leave entitlement itself and invest in the transitions on either side: the ramp-down before leave and the phased return afterwards. These are often where the most significant stress and attrition risk occur, and relatively low-cost interventions – such as temporary workload adjustments and reintegration plans – can make a meaningful difference.

Build in peer support structures alongside formal HR provision. Many employees find it easier to ask a colleague who has been through a similar experience than to raise concerns with a manager or HR partner. Buddy systems and staff networks are a cost-effective way to provide that layer of informal support, and they help build a culture where parenthood is openly acknowledged and accommodated.
